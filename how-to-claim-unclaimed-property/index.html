How to Claim Unclaimed Property: The Complete 2025 Process

Filing an unclaimed property claim isn’t hard, but the documentation requirements and timeline catch most first-time claimants off-guard. Here is the exact, step-by-step process that works in every U.S. state, what to do when you hit the common roadblocks, and when it makes sense to hire help.

Reviewed by David Dorfman, CEO & President, CollectRefund · Last updated May 8, 2026

Step 1: Search every state where you’ve lived or worked

Property is held by the state, not by you. If you’ve moved across state lines, worked for an out-of-state employer, or had stock or insurance with a company headquartered elsewhere, you may have unclaimed property in multiple states. Always search:

  • Every state you’ve lived in (including college and military addresses)
  • Every state you’ve worked in (including remote roles paid out of an HQ state)
  • The HQ state of every former employer, brokerage, and bank
  • Every state your parents and grandparents lived in if claiming inherited property

Use MissingMoney.com for a multi-state sweep, then verify hits in each individual state’s official portal, the state portals update first and carry the authoritative record.

Step 2: Search every name variation

The single biggest reason real matches get missed:

  • name variations. Search every combination of: First name, full middle name, middle initial, no middle name
  • Maiden name, married name, hyphenated combinations, prior married names
  • Common misspellings of unusual surnames
  • Nicknames you used on financial accounts (Bill / William, Liz / Elizabeth)
  • Suffix variations: Jr., Sr., II, III

Step 3: Document the match

Before initiating a claim, capture proof of the listing:

  • Screenshot the search result with date and URL visible
  • Note the property ID, holder name, last known address, and reported amount (if shown)
  • Check whether the state shows the property as cash, securities, or safe deposit contents: each has a different process

Step 4: Gather your claim packet

Most states require a baseline packet plus extras based on claim type and value.

  • Always required: Government-issued photo ID (driver’s license or passport); Social Security number, for state cross-verification (never enter on a third-party form first); Proof of association with the listed address, utility bill, bank statement, tax return, lease, or pay stub from that period.
  • For inherited / estate claims: Death certificate (certified copy); Will + letters testamentary, OR letters of administration; Proof of heirship for each heir (birth certificates, marriage certificates); If the estate is closed: a small estate affidavit or court order may be required.
  • For business / institutional claims: Articles of incorporation / formation; EIN documentation; Proof of signing authority (corporate resolution, operating agreement); For dissolved entities: dissolution paperwork plus successor or shareholder records.
  • For securities claims: Original purchase confirmations or stock certificates if you have them; Documentation of any corporate actions (mergers, splits, name changes) since the property was reported; Brokerage statements showing prior holdings.
  • For higher-value claims (typically $5,000+): A notarized claim affidavit, most states require this above a defined dollar threshold; Additional address-history documentation.

Step 5: File the claim through the official portal

Every state runs its own claim portal. Some support fully online filing for cash claims under a threshold (often $1,000–$2,500). Larger or non-cash claims are still document-driven, you upload the packet through the portal and the state mails or emails follow-up requests.

Always file through the state’s official URL, not a third-party site. Each of our state guides links directly to the official portal for that state.

Step 6: Track and respond to state requests

After submission, the state assigns a claim number. Expect:

  • Acknowledgement within 1–4 weeks
  • First substantive review 30–90 days
  • Supplemental document request — common, respond within the deadline (usually 30–60 days) or the claim auto-closes
  • Approval and disbursement 60–120 days for simple cash
  • 6–12 months for estate, securities, business, or multi-state claims

Step 7: Receive payment

States pay by paper check by default; some support ACH for in-state claimants. For securities claims, the state typically liquidates the position at current market value and pays cash, unless you specifically requested in-kind transfer (rules vary by state).

Common roadblocks and how to handle them

  • Address gap — You can’t prove you lived at the address on file. Pull old tax returns (the IRS keeps 7 years on file via Get Transcript), old W-2s, voter registration records, or DMV history. For older claims, a contemporaneous utility account record is usually accepted.
  • Name change without paperwork — Marriage certificate or court name-change order is required. Lost? Order a certified copy from the county clerk where it was issued.
  • Heirs disagree on filing — File as a single representative with a written agreement among heirs, or open a small probate proceeding to formalize representative authority.
  • Claim denied — States generally allow refiling with new evidence. Read the denial reason carefully, most denials are documentation gaps, not eligibility issues.
  • Securities sold at the wrong time — Some states allow appeal for in-kind reinstatement if the security was liquidated at a substantial loss vs. current market value, but the rules are tight and the window is short.

Should you file yourself or hire help?

File it yourself when:

  • The claim is a small cash amount (under $1,000–$2,500)
  • You’re the listed owner and your address history is clean
  • You can produce ID + proof-of-address documents quickly

Consider CollectRefund when the claim is:

  • Inherited / estate with multiple heirs or out-of-state beneficiaries
  • Securities with corporate-action history since escheatment
  • Business or institutional, especially for dissolved or merged entities
  • Multi-state across two or more state programs
  • Higher-value ($5,000+) where notarization and stricter documentation kicks in
  • Previously denied: needs a refiling strategy
